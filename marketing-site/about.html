Made in New Zealand

By a Kiwi chaser, for Kiwi chasers.

Spot The Aurora isn't a global app with a New Zealand setting bolted on. It was built here, for our latitudes, our skies and our southern lights — by someone who is out there in the cold with you.

Why it exists

It started with the drive home. You check a forecast, you see a number that sounds promising, you pack the car, you drive an hour or two to a dark spot with a clear southern horizon — and there's nothing. Or there's cloud. Or the moon is up and what's there is washed out to a faint grey smudge.

The information needed to avoid every one of those wasted trips already existed. It was just scattered across a dozen government websites, in units nobody outside the field uses, updating at different rates, none of it referenced to New Zealand, and none of it answering the only question that matters: can I see it, from here, tonight, with what I've got?

So the pieces got pulled together. Solar wind from the L1 satellites. Auroral power from the GOES spacecraft. A magnetometer in Canterbury that knows when the electrojet is overhead. Cloud cover. The moon. Where you actually are. And then the hard part — turning all of it into one honest sentence anybody can act on.

Then it kept growing, because once the data was in one place it became obvious what else was possible: watching CMEs cross the solar system in 3D, finding coronal holes on the live solar disk, showing the magnetosphere loading and snapping. Things that, as far as we know, no other public aurora app does at all.

Who's behind it

Spot The Aurora is built and run by TNR Protography, a New Zealand astrophotography outfit based on the West Coast of the South Island. Not a company, not a startup, not a team — one photographer who wanted a better tool and then wouldn't stop improving it.

Everything in the app comes from actually being out there: the visibility tiers exist because "will it show on my phone or do I need the big camera" is the question chasers genuinely ask; the moon gating exists because of specific wasted nights; night mode exists because checking your phone at a dark site ruins twenty minutes of dark adaptation. It's a chaser's tool, designed by someone who uses it every clear night.

Feature requests from users have shaped a large part of it. If something's missing or wrong, say so — it genuinely does get built.

☕ Support

Free forever. Kept alive by coffee.

There are no ads, no subscriptions, no paywalled features and no data being sold. There never will be. But the domain, the hosting, the edge workers and the weather API calls all cost real money every month, and every single person who uses the app adds to that.
