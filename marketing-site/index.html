Why it exists

It started with
the drive home.

You check a forecast, see a number that sounds promising, pack the car and drive out to somewhere dark with a clear view south. Then there's nothing. Or cloud. Or the moon is up and whatever is there has been washed out to a faint grey smudge.

The information needed to avoid every one of those nights already existed. It was just scattered across a dozen government websites, in units nobody outside the field uses, updating at different rates, none of it referenced to New Zealand, and none of it answering the only question that actually matters.

Can I see it, from here, tonight, with what I've got?

"Kp 5 tonight" tells you almost nothing

Kp is a planetary average, published three hours after the fact. It says nothing about where you are, what the sky above you is doing, or whether the aurora will be bright enough for your eyes rather than a 20 second exposure.

Chasers have been filling in those gaps by hand for years. This does it for you.

Eye, phone, or camera?

The single most useful question, and almost nothing answers it. The score is tied to real thresholds: nothing, long exposure only, phone night mode, or genuinely visible to the naked eye.

The moon gets a veto

A faint display under a bright moon is a wasted drive. The forecast accounts for moon illumination, so a score on a bright night means something different to the same score under a new moon.

Your location, not "New Zealand"

Invercargill and Whangarei do not get the same aurora. Grant location access and the score adjusts for your latitude, using where the auroral oval sits relative to your horizon.

Things nothing else does

Three of these exist
nowhere else.

Not features borrowed off a NOAA page. Built from scratch, running live, and as far as anyone has been able to tell us, not available in any other public aurora tool.

01. A live 3D model of the solar system, with the CMEs in flight

Watch coronal mass ejections leave the Sun and travel toward Earth in a real, interactive 3D heliosphere. Every CME in NASA's catalogue is propagated with published drag physics, so a fast one genuinely slows down as it ploughs into slower wind ahead of it, instead of sliding along in a straight line at a fixed speed.

  • Scrub the timeline forward and back, seven days of history plus the forecast window.
  • Colour tracks current speed, grey near ambient wind through to pink for the extreme events.
  • Click a flare on the Solar Activity page and jump straight to the CME it launched.
  • A seven day impact forecast projects what arrives at Earth and roughly when.

02. Coronal holes, found live in your browser

Coronal holes fire high speed streams at Earth and cause a large share of New Zealand's aurora. Most apps ignore them completely. This one finds them. It pulls the latest image of the Sun and runs a ten step detection pipeline, limb detection through to heliographic projection, entirely on your device with no server doing the work.

  • Detected holes are drawn on the solar disk at their real position and size.
  • Parker spiral arms project the resulting stream out past Earth's orbit, so you can see what's coming.
  • 72 hours of history is tracked, so you can watch a hole rotate into a position that points at us.

03. The magnetosphere, actually visualised

The physics that decides whether you see anything is usually left as jargon on a chart. Here it's performed live, driven by the real measurements coming off the satellites.

  • The magnetotail scene. Solar wind particles stream in at the measured speed, in numbers tracking the measured density. Northward field and they visibly deflect around the magnetosphere. Southward and they slip in, spiral down the tail and pile up as a growing glow. Then the substorm fires: a flash at the pinch point, a river of particles onto the pole, curtains blooming on the globe. A five step tour walks you through the whole chain.
  • The solar wind structure cross section. An animated slice of whatever is passing us right now, shock front, CME sheath, magnetic cloud, stream interaction region, with particles moving at the real measured speed.
  • The Russell McPherron effect, live. The seasonal geometry that makes equinox aurora better, reduced to a plain helping, neutral or working against you meter, with the full geometry there if you want it.
  • The IMF clock angle dial. Watch the field vector rotate in real time. Six o'clock is maximum coupling, and the strongest aurora signature there is.

The whole chain, one app

From the surface of the Sun
to the sky above your house.

Most tools give you one link in the chain. This gives you all of it, live, in one place. When something happens on the Sun, you can follow it the whole way to your back paddock.

1. On the Sun

X-ray flux with every flare marked and clickable, sunspot regions mapped onto live solar imagery with their flare probabilities, proton flux, coronal imagery and coronagraph video.

2. In flight

CMEs crossing the 3D heliosphere with real drag physics, coronal hole streams drawn along Parker spirals, and a seven day forecast of what reaches Earth and when.

3. At L1

NASA's IMAP and NOAA's DSCOVR sit 1.5 million km upsun, which buys roughly 45 to 60 minutes of warning. Speed, density, temperature and the all important Bz, updated every minute.

4. On the ground here

GeoNet's Eyrewell observatory in Canterbury measures the field changing over New Zealand itself. A sharp bay means the electrojet is overhead now. This is why the score can differ from global Kp.

5. Eyes on the sky

Then the part no satellite can give you. Other people, out there right now, pinning reports to a map. Naked eye, phone, camera, nothing, or clouded out, feeding straight back into the Now slot of the forecast. Plus cloud cover from Windy and seven live cameras from Oban to New Plymouth, so you can look at the sky before you commit to the drive.

Built for everyone looking south

One app, two views, one tap between them. Nobody gets talked down to and nobody hits a ceiling.

New to this

Simple View. A percentage, a sentence in plain English, and four time slots that say things like "conditions are building". No jargon, no acronyms. Look at the map, see what people are reporting, check the cloud, go outside.

Been chasing for years

Advanced View. Bz, Bt, By, Bx, speed, density, temperature, hemispheric power, Newell coupling, the Eyrewell trace, the disturbance index, substorm probabilities, clock angle, and 24 hours of history behind every chart.

Visiting the country

You've got a handful of nights and no idea what's normal here. It tells you whether tonight is worth staying up for, what the sky looks like at seven camera sites around the country, and whether the moon is going to ruin it.

Works on whatever you've got

Phone in the car.
Tablet on the couch.
Desktop at home.

It's a proper progressive web app, so there's no app store, no download, no account and no waiting for an update to be approved. Open it in your browser and it works. Add it to your home screen and it behaves like a native app, full screen with its own icon.

  • iPhone and iPad. Safari, then Share, then Add to Home Screen.
  • Android. Chrome, then Install app.
  • Desktop. Works full size in any modern browser, and the 3D scene and the charts are built for the extra room.
  • No account, ever. Your location is used on your device. It is never logged or sold.
