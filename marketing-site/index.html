The problem with every other aurora app

"Kp 5 tonight!" tells you almost nothing.

Kp is a planetary average, published three hours late, that says nothing about where you are, what the sky above you is doing, or whether the aurora will actually be bright enough for your eyes rather than a 20-second exposure. Chasers have been filling in those gaps by hand for years. This app does it for you.

🕶️

Eye, phone, or camera?

The single most useful question, and almost nothing answers it. Spot The Aurora scores visibility on a 0–100% scale tied to real thresholds: nothing, long-exposure camera only, phone night mode, or genuinely visible to the naked eye.

🌕

The moon will blow it out

A faint display under a bright moon is a wasted drive. The forecast and the alerts both account for moon illumination — camera-tier alerts are suppressed entirely above 80% moon, and the naked-eye threshold tightens as the moon brightens.

📍

Your location, not "New Zealand"

Invercargill and Whangārei do not get the same aurora. Grant GPS and the score adjusts for your latitude, and alerts fire based on where the auroral oval actually sits relative to your geomagnetic horizon.

World firsts

Things no other public aurora app does.

These aren't bullet points borrowed from a NOAA page. They are built from scratch, run live, and as far as we know, exist nowhere else in a publicly available aurora tool.

01. A live 3D model of the solar system, with the CMEs in flight

Watch coronal mass ejections leave the Sun and travel toward Earth in a real, interactive 3D heliosphere. Every CME in NASA's DONKI catalogue is propagated with a physics-based drag model (Vršnak et al. 2013), so it slows down realistically instead of moving in a straight line at a fixed speed.

  • Scrub the timeline forward and back — 7 days of history plus the forecast window.
  • Colour tracks current speed, from grey ambient wind through to pink for extreme events.
  • Click a flare on the Solar Activity page and jump straight to its linked CME in the 3D scene.
  • A 7-day impact forecast projects speed and density arriving at Earth.

02. Coronal holes, detected live in your browser

Coronal holes fire high-speed solar wind streams at Earth and cause a large share of New Zealand's aurora. Most apps ignore them completely. This one finds them: it pulls the latest GOES-19 SUVI 195Å image of the Sun and runs a ten-step detection pipeline — limb detection, adaptive thresholding, flood-fill, heliographic projection — entirely on your device, with no server doing the work.

  • Detected holes are drawn on the solar disk with their real position and size.
  • Parker spiral arms project the resulting stream out past Earth's orbit, so you can see what's coming and roughly when.
  • 72 hours of detection history is tracked, so you can see a hole rotate into a geoeffective position.

03. The magnetosphere, actually visualised

The physics that decides whether you see anything — energy loading into the magnetotail, the snap of a substorm, what kind of solar wind structure is passing — is usually left as jargon on a chart. Here it's performed live, driven by the real measurements.

  • Magnetotail scene: solar wind particles stream in at the measured speed, in numbers tracking the measured density. Northward Bz and they visibly deflect around the magnetosphere; southward and they slip in, spiral down the tail and pile up as a growing glow. Then the substorm fires: a flash at the pinch point, a river of particles onto the pole, curtains blooming on the globe. A five-step guided tour walks you through the whole causal chain.
  • Solar wind structure cross-section: an animated slice of the actual structure passing us right now — shock front, CME sheath, magnetic cloud, stream interaction region — with particles moving at the real measured speed.
  • Russell–McPherron effect, live: the seasonal geometry that makes equinox aurora better, reduced to a plain "helping / neutral / working against you" meter, with the full Hapgood 1992 geometry available if you want it.
  • IMF clock angle dial: see the field vector rotate in real time. Six o'clock is maximum coupling — the strongest aurora signature there is.

The whole chain, one app

From the surface of the Sun to the sky above your house.

Every other tool gives you one link in the chain. Spot The Aurora gives you all five, live, in one place — so when something happens on the Sun, you can follow it the whole way to your backyard.

☀️

1. On the Sun

X-ray flux with every flare marked and clickable, sunspot regions mapped onto live SDO/HMI imagery with flare probabilities, proton flux, SUVI coronal imagery and CCOR-1 coronagraph video.

🚀

2. In flight

CMEs propagating through the 3D heliosphere with drag physics, coronal-hole streams drawn along Parker spirals, and a seven-day forecast of what arrives at Earth and when.

🛸️

3. At L1

NASA's IMAP and NOAA's DSCOVR sit 1.5 million km upsun, giving roughly 45–60 minutes of warning. Speed, density, temperature, Bt and the all-important Bz — merged, labelled by source, updated every minute.

🌎

4. On the ground in NZ

GeoNet's Eyrewell observatory in Canterbury measures the magnetic field changing over New Zealand itself. A sharp bay means the electrojet is overhead now — this is why the NZ score can differ from global Kp.

👤

5. Eyes on the sky

And then the part no satellite can give you: other Kiwis, out there right now. Live sighting reports pinned to a map — naked eye, phone, camera, nothing, or clouded out — feeding straight back into the "Now" forecast. Plus cloud cover from Windy and seven live cameras from Oban to New Plymouth, so you can see the sky before you commit to the drive.

Alerts that are worth keeping on

Your phone buzzes when you can see it.

Most aurora alerts are a blunt instrument: one threshold, blasted at an entire country, ignoring cloud, moon and the fact that Invercargill and Auckland are 1,400 km apart. People turn them off within a week.

These are different. For visibility alerts, the app works out where the auroral oval's equatorward boundary actually sits, converts your GPS position into geomagnetic latitude, and only notifies you when the oval reaches your horizon — at the tier that matters.

  • Three visibility tiers: camera, phone, naked eye — and they only escalate, so you're not pinged twice for the same thing.
  • Moon-aware: thresholds tighten as the moon brightens; camera-tier alerts stop entirely above 80% illumination.
  • Overnight watch: one evening summary, at the standard you choose — every night, or only when it's camera, phone, or naked-eye worthy.
  • Event alerts: M-class and above flares, plus all four classes of solar wind shock arriving at L1.
  • Every category is opt-in, with cooldowns so a fluctuating night doesn't wake you six times.

Works everywhere you do

Phone in the car. Tablet on the couch. Desktop at home.

It's a proper progressive web app, so there's no app store, no download, no account, and no waiting for an update to be approved. Open it in your browser and it works. Add it to your home screen and it behaves like a native app — full screen, own icon, offline shell, push notifications.

  • iPhone and iPad: Safari → Share → Add to Home Screen.
  • Android: Chrome → Install app.
  • Desktop: works full-size in any modern browser, and the 3D scene and charts are built for the extra room.
  • No account, ever. Your location is used on your device and for your alerts. It is never logged or sold.
