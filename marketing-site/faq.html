Getting started

Is it really free?

Yes. Free, no ads, no subscription, no account, no premium tier withholding features. Every single thing described on this site is available to everyone. It's paid for out of pocket and supported by people who choose to shout a coffee. That's entirely optional and changes nothing in the app.

Do I need to download it from an app store?

No. It's a progressive web app — open spottheaurora.co.nz in your browser and it works immediately, on phone, tablet or desktop.

To get the full app experience with push notifications, add it to your home screen. On iPhone or iPad: open it in Safari, tap Share, then Add to Home Screen. On Android: open it in Chrome and tap Install app. It then runs full screen with its own icon, just like a store app.

I tapped the link in Facebook and it won't let me install it.

Facebook and Instagram open links in their own in-app browser, which can't install web apps. Tap the ••• or ⋮ menu and choose Open in Browser (Safari) or Open in Chrome, then install from there.

Do I have to give it my location?

No, but it's much more useful if you do. Without it you get the reference forecast for Greymouth on the West Coast. With it, your score is adjusted for your latitude and your alerts fire based on whether the aurora is visible from where you are.

Your position is used on your device and by the alert system. It is never logged, sold or shared with a third party.

Using the forecast

What do the percentages actually mean?

They're a visibility estimate, not a probability of the aurora existing. Roughly: under 20% nothing, 20–35% long-exposure camera only, 35–50% your phone's night mode will catch it, 50–65% a faint glow from a dark site, 65–80% visible to the naked eye, and 80%+ get outside now.

Those are referenced to the South Island. Dark skies and a clear southern horizon matter enormously — a 45% night from a genuinely dark site beats a 60% night from a suburban backyard.

Why is your score different from the Kp forecast everyone else quotes?

Because Kp is a planetary average published three hours after the fact. It tells you roughly how disturbed the whole Earth was, not what's happening over New Zealand in the next fifteen minutes.

This app uses live solar wind measured at L1 about 45–60 minutes upstream of us, a direct auroral power measurement from the GOES satellites, and a magnetometer in Canterbury that responds within a minute to a substorm current overhead. When those disagree with a stale Kp number, they're usually the ones worth believing.

How far ahead can it forecast?

Honestly: about an hour with real confidence. The solar wind is measured 1.5 million km upsun, which buys roughly 45–60 minutes of warning. The Now and 15-minute slots are the reliable ones; 30 minutes is medium confidence; the 60-minute slot is guidance — read it as "conditions are building" rather than a promise.

Further out than that, the CME visualisation is your tool. It shows what's in flight days ahead, though arrival times always carry hours of uncertainty.

The score is high but I can't see anything. What gives?

Usually one of four things. Cloud — check the cloud overlay and the live cameras. Moon — a bright moon washes out a faint display; check the moon arc. Light pollution or a hill to the south — you need a dark site with a clear southern horizon. Or dark adaptation — give your eyes a full twenty minutes away from your phone screen before you decide there's nothing there. Turn on night mode in the app so checking it doesn't reset that.

It's also worth taking a 10-second phone exposure pointed south. Cameras see aurora long before eyes do, and it's often already there.

What's a substorm, and why does the app make such a fuss about it?

It's the sudden burst that most New Zealand aurora actually consists of. Energy loads into Earth's magnetic tail while the solar wind field points south, the tail stretches, and eventually it snaps — firing particles into the atmosphere and lighting up the sky in minutes.

The app estimates the probability of that happening in the next 30 and 60 minutes, and separately detects when one has already started by watching the Canterbury magnetometer for the ground signature. That's your cue to be outside with your eyes adjusted, not still deciding.

Alerts

Why did my friend get an alert and I didn't?

Because they can see it and you can't — that's the entire point. Visibility alerts are calculated per person: the app works out where the auroral oval's edge is, converts your GPS position to geomagnetic latitude, and only notifies you when the aurora reaches your horizon, at the tier that applies to you. Someone 300 km further south gets a better alert, or gets one when you don't.

Will it wake me up constantly?

No. Every category is opt-in. Visibility alerts only escalate — once you've had the camera-tier alert you won't get it again unless conditions improve to the next tier up. Each tier has a two-hour cooldown per person, shock alerts have four hours, and the overnight summary is capped at one per day. And on a bright-moon night the lower tiers switch off entirely.

What's the overnight watch?

A single evening summary between 6 and 9pm NZ time telling you whether tonight is worth staying up for. You set the bar: every night regardless, or only when conditions reach camera, phone, or naked-eye standard. One notification per day, maximum.

I turned on notifications and nothing's happening.

Check three things. First, that you installed the app to your home screen — on iOS especially, push only works for an installed web app, not a Safari tab. Second, that notifications are allowed for it in your phone's system settings. Third, that conditions have actually warranted an alert since you subscribed — on a quiet week, silence is correct behaviour.

Seeing the aurora in New Zealand

Where's the best place to see it?

Anywhere in the South Island with a dark, unobstructed view to the south — the further south the better. Southland, Otago, Stewart Island and the Catlins are exceptional. The West Coast, Canterbury and the Mackenzie Basin are all excellent. On a strong night the aurora is visible from the North Island too, and during major storms it's been seen from Northland.

The essentials are a southern horizon over water or flat land, distance from town lights, and clear sky. Use the cloud overlay to find the gap.

When's the best time of year?

Around the equinoxes — March and September — geomagnetic activity is measurably higher thanks to the Russell–McPherron effect, which the app shows you live. Winter gives you the longest dark hours. Realistically, the best time is whenever there's activity and clear sky, which is exactly what the alerts are for.

Aurora tends to peak around local magnetic midnight, roughly 10pm to 2am, though a good substorm can fire at any time after dark.

What camera settings should I use?

Starting point for a proper camera: widest aperture you have (f/2.8 or faster), ISO 3200–6400, 8–15 second exposure, manual focus on a bright star, on a tripod. Adjust from there.

On a modern phone, night mode pointed south on a stable surface will pick up more than you expect — often the fastest way to confirm whether anything's there.

The project

Who built this?

Dean at TNR Protography — a New Zealand astrophotographer on the West Coast who got tired of driving hours on unreliable forecasts and built the tool he wanted. It's made in New Zealand, for New Zealand conditions. More about the project →

Can I use it outside New Zealand?

Partly. The solar data — flares, CMEs, solar wind, the 3D visualisation, coronal holes — is global and works anywhere. The aurora score, the ground magnetometer, the sightings map and the cameras are all built around New Zealand and the southern lights, so they're most useful here. Southern Australia gets reasonable value from it too.

Is it an official forecast?

No. It's a chaser's tool and a citizen-science project — a rigorous one built on the same public data the professionals use, but not an official warning service. For critical infrastructure or official space weather warnings, use NOAA SWPC and MetService NZ.

I found a bug, or I've got an idea.

Both very welcome. Email help@spottheaurora.co.nz or post on Facebook. A good chunk of what's in the app came from users asking for it. The change log shows what's shipped recently.
